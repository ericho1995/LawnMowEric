Mowing technique

How to mow your lawn like you mean it

Most lawn problems trace back to how they're mowed, not how often. Get the technique right and half the other issues on this page stop showing up in the first place.

Follow the one-third rule

Never cut off more than a third of the blade length in a single pass. Take more than that and the plant loses too much of its leaf area at once — it responds by pushing energy into regrowing leaf instead of roots, which is exactly backwards from what you want. If a lawn's gotten away from you, bring it down gradually over two or three mows a few days apart rather than scalping it back in one go.

Mow to the grass type, not a fixed number

Buffalo lawns want to be kept taller — down around 30mm and it thins out fast and lets weeds in. Couch and kikuyu handle a shorter cut and actually thicken up when kept low and mowed often. If you're not sure what's growing in your yard, mow on the higher side until you do; it's much easier to lower a healthy lawn than to fix a scalped one.

A few habits that make a bigger difference than people expect

  • Mow when the grass is dry — wet clippings clump, smother the lawn underneath, and drag on the mower.
  • Change direction each visit. Mowing the same stripes every time compacts the soil in the same wheel tracks and can train the grass to lean.
  • Keep the blade sharp. A dull blade tears the grass tip instead of cutting it cleanly — torn tips brown off and are an easy entry point for fungal disease.
  • Leave short clippings where they fall. They break down fast and return nitrogen to the soil. Only bag up if the lawn was overgrown and the clippings are clumping.
Warning signs

5 signs your lawn is asking for help

A lawn rarely fails overnight — it gives warning first. Catching these early is the difference between a quick fix and pulling up half the yard.

  • Yellow or pale patches spreading outward. Could be a fungal disease, a nutrient gap, or dog urine burn if it's a single sharp-edged spot. A patch that keeps growing week to week is worth acting on rather than watching.
  • Thinning grass with bare soil showing through. Usually compacted soil (heavy foot traffic, no aeration) or, in warmer months, curl grub and lawn armyworm damage underneath.
  • Weeds taking over one particular area. Weeds are usually a symptom, not the cause — they move in wherever the grass is already thin or stressed. Fixing the lawn's health there does more long-term than just pulling weeds.
  • A spongy, springy feel underfoot. That's thatch — a dead-organic-matter layer built up between the soil and the green growth. A thin layer is normal; a thick one blocks water and nutrients from reaching the roots.
  • Grass that doesn't spring back after being walked on. If footprints are still visible an hour later, the lawn is telling you it needs water, or the soil beneath it is too compacted for the roots to bounce back properly.
Watering

Watering, done right

More lawns are damaged by the wrong watering habits than by too little water. The goal isn't a wet lawn every day — it's healthy roots.

Deep and infrequent beats little and often

A quick daily sprinkle only wets the top few centimetres of soil, which trains roots to stay shallow near the surface. Watering less often but for longer sends moisture deeper, which pulls roots down with it — and deep roots cope with heat and dry spells far better.

Time of day matters

Early morning, before around 9am, is the best window — the lawn gets to drink before the day heats up and evaporation takes over. Avoid evening watering where possible; grass that stays damp overnight is much more prone to fungal disease.

How much is enough?

As a rough guide, Melbourne lawns want somewhere around 25mm of water a week through summer, topped up with less (or nothing) after real rain. An easy way to check: push a screwdriver into the lawn after watering. If it slides in easily to 10-15cm, you've watered deeply enough. If it stops well short of that, the water hasn't gotten past the surface.

Year-round health

Small habits that keep a lawn healthy all year

None of this needs to be a big production. A handful of things done at the right time of year matter far more than any single one done perfectly.

  • Aerate compacted soil. Spring or autumn, once a year on high-traffic lawns. Aerating punches small holes through the soil so water, air and nutrients can actually reach the roots instead of pooling on a hardened surface.
  • Feed little and often, not one big hit. A light feed a few times through the growing season keeps growth steady. A single heavy dose tends to produce a quick flush of soft, weak growth that's more prone to disease.
  • Keep thatch in check. A light dethatch or scarify when the spongy layer starts building up stops it from smothering the lawn underneath.
  • Overseed thin patches in the right season. Bare or thinning areas fill back in far faster with a bit of help than left to recover on their own.
  • Walk the lawn between mows. The five warning signs above are far easier to deal with caught in week one than week six.
